Much of the work below takes place through the [Toronto Climate Observatory](http://climateobservatory.ca) (TCO), which I lead. The TCO is a community of interdisciplinary scholars from across all three University of Toronto campuses developing new approaches to climate informatics — the data, methods, and relationships that local efforts to mitigate and adapt to climate change actually require. We work alongside municipal governments, community organizations, and advocacy groups rather than at a distance from them.

This is a partial list of current activities. Refer to my [Google Scholar Profile](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=1vePPCkAAAAJ&hl=en) for a complete list of publications.

### Climate Data and Governance in the Greater Toronto Area

How is climate data produced, contested, and put to use in a large city? This strand studies the practices of the people who make and rely on climate information in the GTA — municipal staff, grassroots activists, and community organizations — and builds tools and methods to support them. Recent work includes an analysis of the emissions financed by Toronto's investment industry, which contributed to a Toronto City Council motion on the issue, and an ongoing collaboration with the Clean Air Partnership on public flood data for Southern Ontario.

### Human-Centered Design for Weather and Disaster Risk Communication

As the climate crisis accelerates, forecasts and warnings that were once translated for the public by emergency managers and broadcast meteorologists are increasingly accessed directly, by audiences they were never designed for. This project asks who is left out by current risk communication practice and what it would take to include them. With funding from the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, we studied how members of the public seek and interpret hurricane risk information, producing guidance now being taken up in NOAA's digital risk communication strategy. A subsequent SSHRC-funded study, conducted with York Emergency Management and a Toronto blind service organization, examined the severe weather information needs of people with visual impairments. We are currently extending this agenda to people living with chronic illnesses that make them susceptible to extreme heat.

### Infrastructuring Informal and Community-Based Responses to Disaster

During crises, affected communities improvise remarkably with the technologies at hand. These informal responses are routinely overlooked by the researchers and designers building emergency management technology, and unaccounted for in the preparations of formal responders. Beginning with an ethnographic study of the mutual aid networks that emerged across New York City in the first months of the COVID-19 pandemic, this line of work documents how community organizations actually do crisis response, and what better support for them would look like. Since 2021 I have worked with collaborators and community organizations in Puerto Rico — one of the most disaster-prone regions in the Western Hemisphere, and one where community-level action is a necessary but largely unsupported part of all crisis response. With funding from the US National Science Foundation and the Natural Hazards Center, our team co-designed, prototyped, and piloted a web and mobile platform now in use across ten communities on the island.

### Peer Production and the Sustainability of Open Geospatial Data

Before starting my PhD I helped found the [Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team](http://hotosm.org) and launched the [Open Data for Resilience Initiative](http://opendri.org) at the World Bank, both of which depended on volunteers producing geospatial data that institutions could actually rely on. That work left me with a question I have kept returning to: what makes an open mapping community durable? This strand studies OpenStreetMap empirically — how contributors enter and leave the project, what happens when corporate editing teams begin working alongside volunteers, and how the community negotiates tensions between openness, membership, and policy. A 2026 SSHRC Insight Grant, *Sustaining the Map: A Mixed-Methods Intervention for OpenStreetMap Contributor Retention* ($465,062), moves this from description toward intervention, testing what actually helps contributors stay.

### Critical & Interdisciplinary Perspectives on Disaster and Climate Informatics  

Calls for interdisciplinary collaboration on climate change and other "wicked problems" have been made for decades, but in practice such collaborations remain difficult and often unsuccessful. This work treats the collaboration itself as an object of study and design. With collaborators, I organized month-long interdisciplinary "Field Labs" on climate data in Chiang Mai and Kathmandu, each drawing over 150 scholars, practitioners, and students from fields ranging from machine learning and hydrology to anthropology and art. Through ethnographic observation and follow-up interviews we showed how participatory and human-centered design methods can be used to stage interdisciplinary encounters more productively. A related strand argues for taking history seriously in HCI — attending to the social, technological, and political contexts that shape how crises unfold, rather than studying only the moment of disaster itself.

### Accounting for Equity in Disaster and Climate Risk Modeling

Data science and machine learning stand to improve how we evaluate and mitigate climate and disaster risk, but early experiments in the field have not gone without problems, and the application of these methods in sensitive contexts is fraught with issues of bias, transparency, and harm. With funding from the World Bank, I convened a working group of more than sixty scholars and practitioners working at the intersection of data science and disaster risk management, producing the first industry report on responsible AI for the field. Subsequent work with a SSHRC Partnership Engage Grant and the University of Toronto Data Sciences Institute identified and evaluated seventy-three risk assessments conducted by major international development organizations over the last decade, yielding a typology of approaches to modeling distributional inequity. We found that very few risk assessments account for equity effectively — with the consequence that the mitigation and adaptation measures they inform may inadvertently sustain or worsen social inequalities.
